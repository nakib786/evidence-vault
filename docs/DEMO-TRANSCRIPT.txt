[00:00] Someone shouts abuse at a woman on a bus. Someone posts a threat
about a mosque. By the time anyone reports it, the post is deleted - or
the video is just a file on a phone that anyone could have edited.

[00:12] This is Evidence Vault. It turns what you saw into a record that
still holds up weeks later, without handing your evidence to anyone.

[00:20] There's a short walkthrough the first time you open it. You can
skip it - nobody should have to sit through a product tour to report what
just happened to them.

[00:35] Most of what this documents is spoken, not written. So the main
way in is video, with sound - because the words are usually the evidence.
It's fingerprinted the moment you stop recording, before it's ever a file
that could be edited.

[01:00] That fingerprint is a SHA-256 hash. Change a single frame and it
changes completely.

[01:08] Then the fingerprint - and only the fingerprint - goes to four
independent OpenTimestamps calendar servers, which register it on a
public blockchain ledger. No cryptocurrency changes hands. Your
recording never goes anywhere.

[01:20] The person using this just lived through it. So the recording
stays blurred until they choose to look, and they never have to look again
to finish. Every field is optional.

[01:45] They choose the category themselves. We ship no AI classifier -
the rules forbid sending hateful material to a third-party service, and
the obvious model is text-only with no Arabic or Urdu support anyway.

[01:56] That multilingual capability went here instead: on-device text
recognition in English, Arabic and Urdu, running in the browser.

[02:05] Then the part most tools skip. Where does this actually go?

[02:12] No police force or court in the US or Canada accepts evidence
through an automated submission - so we don't pretend to file one.
Instead: tell it where you are, and it shows who takes these reports and
how.

[02:25] That "how" matters. Most police services have no online hate-crime
form at all. The NYPD tells you to call your precinct. Chicago publishes a
phone number. So phone and web are shown side by side, and each one says
what it will actually accept.

[02:40] And for a lawyer or a court, it generates a certificate of
authenticity - written to Federal Rules of Evidence 902, or the Canada
Evidence Act, depending on where you are. Those rules let electronic
records be authenticated by hash value instead of live testimony, which is
exactly what this app already produces.

[03:00] The export is five files: the report, the original recording, the
proof, the certificate, and a plain-text cover letter you can paste
straight into a reporting form.

[03:15] Here's what matters most. I'm verifying with the official
OpenTimestamps client - not our code.

[03:25] The fingerprint matches this exact file, and the file existed no
later than that timestamp. Anyone can run that check, anywhere, and it
keeps working even if this project disappears tomorrow.

[03:45] No account. No database. No analytics. Closing the tab deletes
everything, because there was never anywhere else for it to be.

[03:52] A timestamp proves when a file existed and that it hasn't changed -
not that its contents are true. The report says exactly that on its own
final page, so nobody downstream over-reads it.

[03:58] Evidence Vault. Open source, MIT licensed.
